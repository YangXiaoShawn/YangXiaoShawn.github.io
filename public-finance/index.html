Tariffs as a Commodity Tax

A tariff is an ad valorem commodity tax collected at the border. This paper asks of it what public finance asks of any tax: who pays, how the burden varies with income, what a tax on business inputs costs, and whether prices fall when the tax is removed.

It starts from my earlier estimate that the 2018–2019 tariffs were passed through to U.S. importers (Who absorbed the tariff?) and follows the tax from the customs entry, through input–output tables, to households by income decile. It then compares the tariff increases of 2025 with their partial removal after the Supreme Court ruling of February 2026.

Term paper and class presentation for ECON 85100 Public Finance I at the Graduate Center, City University of New York. This is a working draft and the numbers may change.

Findings so far

Four questions, four answers.

Efficiency

Mostly not a tax on consumer goods.

The 2018–2019 tariffs come to $43.1 billion a year at 2017 quantities. Only 21% falls on imports that households buy directly, and 79% of the base is not final consumption. Through domestic production, 44% reaches household consumption.

Against the Diamond–Mirrlees benchmark, a tax on inputs should be zero.

Distribution

Regressive against income, proportional against expenditure.

If fully passed forward, the burden is $146 a year per household: 0.33% of after-tax income in the bottom quintile and 0.11% in the top, but 0.15% to 0.14% of expenditure.

93% of the gap comes from spending relative to income, not from the tariff content of the basket.

Incidence

Beyond the importer, the answer is an interval.

Border data show no evidence that exporters paid. They cannot say how much importers and retailers passed on. With percentage markups consumers pay 1.67 times the tariff; if retail prices barely move, owners of firms pay and the tax is progressive.

Public price data cannot choose between these cases; the paper reports the regression as a non-result.

Symmetry

Symmetry is not rejected, but the test is weak.

Comparing the same product–origin cells before, during and after the 2025 tariffs, the elasticity of the tariff-exclusive price is −0.08 (0.05) on the way up and −0.07 (0.05) on the way down. In cells above $10 million (90% of trade), buyers paid 69% of the increase and got back 83% of the cut.

The smallest asymmetry the test can detect is 0.13 to 0.43. The removal was partial and covers four months.

What is next

Rank households by expenditure with the Consumer Expenditure Survey microdata. Estimate retail pass-through, up and down, from item-level consumer prices in 2025–2026. Use the 2026 refunds to importers as a test of who had paid.

Scope of the evidence. Customs unit values are not transaction prices. The household results are an accounting of where the tax lands under stated pass-through assumptions, not an estimate of those assumptions.
